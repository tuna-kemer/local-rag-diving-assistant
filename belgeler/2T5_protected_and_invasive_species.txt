PROTECTED SPECIES AND INVASIVE SPECIES IN TURKISH SEAS

Species Requiring Protection: As a result of climate change and various human activities (pollution, overfishing, coastal development, etc.), some species in the Mediterranean have declined or begun to disappear in various regions, making it necessary to protect both their habitats and their populations. Invasive alien species also play a role in ecosystem degradation and species decline, such as the green algae Caulerpa taxifolia and Caulerpa racemosa var. cylindracea. Many public and civil society organizations in Mediterranean countries carry out protection and awareness activities related to endangered or threatened species, including Posidonia oceanica seagrass meadows, coastal Cystoseira communities, calcareous algal formations, sponges, corals, mollusks, sea urchins, crustaceans, fish, marine mammals, and sea turtles. These species are also protected under national laws and international agreements.

Marine Flora: Protected marine plant communities include coastal Cystoseira communities, vermetid terraces and/or calcareous algal ridges, and seagrass meadows (Posidonia oceanica, Cymodocea nodosa, Zostera noltii).

Coastal Cystoseira communities include five species (C. amentacea var. stricta, C. amentacea var. spicata, C. spinosa, C. zosteroides, C. sedoides, C. mediterranea) that are protected under various international agreements.

Vermetid terraces and calcareous algal ridges are reef-like structures formed since the late Miocene (5-10 million years ago) by vermetid gastropods together with calcareous algae (such as Lithophyllum byssoides and Corallina species) in the intertidal and wave-exposed zones of the Mediterranean. Depending on the coastline, they form as narrow ridges or wide terraces, hosting diverse algae and animal species. Because they indicate the character of the surrounding water, they should be monitored; they are threatened by climate change, global warming, coastal development, and pollution.

Posidonia oceanica meadows have been called "the tree of life of the Mediterranean." Posidonia oceanica is a flowering marine plant; there are only about sixty species of flowering marine plants worldwide, compared to about twenty thousand species of marine algae, yet seagrass meadows cover areas as ecologically important as algae beds. In the Mediterranean, Posidonia oceanica meadows are distributed between depths of 0-40 meters depending on the region, forming dense underwater "forests." Each square meter can contain around 1,000 leaf bundles, each with about 8 leaves 80-100 cm long. Named after the sea god Poseidon, these plants grow slowly (about 3 cm per year) through horizontal and vertical stems, and thousands of marine plants and animals develop on and around them, which provide feeding, shelter, and breeding grounds. Cymodocea nodosa is rare in the Mediterranean and is considered endangered due to sensitivity to coastal development. Zostera noltii is found on sandy seabeds, river mouths, and lagoons across all Turkish seas and provides shelter for many marine species. Anchoring damages seagrass meadows because dragging an anchor and chain across the seabed harms the substrate and the organisms living on it; guidance documents from Western Europe classify seabeds into five categories (sandy, muddy, gravel, rocky/blocky, shelly, and Posidonia meadow) and recommend anchoring methods suited to each.

Marine Fauna: Numerous groups of Mediterranean marine animals are protected under international agreements, including sponges, cnidarians, mollusks (including cephalopods), crustaceans, echinoderms, fish, marine mammals, and sea turtles. Protected mollusks include the bath sponge (Spongia officinalis), red coral (Paramuricea clavata), and, among more than two thousand Mediterranean mollusk species, 17 species protected under the Bern Convention such as Patella ferruginea and Pinna nobilis, whose collection and trade are banned. Large crustaceans of commercial value, such as lobsters, slipper lobsters, and crabs, face population decline from overfishing and pollution, with examples including the giant slipper lobster (Scyllarides latus), European lobster (Homarus gammarus), and spiny lobster (Palinurus elephas). Among fish, species threatened especially by spearfishing overexploitation include the dusky grouper (Epinephelus marginatus), other grouper species (Epinephelus aeneus, Epinephelus costae), brown meagre (Sciaena umbra), and seahorses (Hippocampus hippocampus, Hippocampus guttulatus).

Marine Mammals: Fifteen marine mammal species have been recorded in Turkish seas to date, including dolphins, whales, and seals. The Mediterranean monk seal (Monachus monachus), a critically endangered pinniped, is the only regularly sighted seal species and is distributed across all Turkish seas, though it is believed to have disappeared from the Black Sea coast in recent decades. Dolphin and whale species recorded include the fin whale (Balaenoptera physalus), sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus), Cuvier's beaked whale (Ziphius cavirostris), common bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus), striped dolphin (Stenella coeruleoalba), short-beaked common dolphin (Delphinus delphis), Risso's dolphin (Grampus griseus), and harbor porpoise (Phocoena phocoena). Marine mammals are threatened by habitat loss and degradation, hunting, accidental bycatch in fishing nets, deliberate killing, pollution, and human disturbance.

Sea Turtles: Three sea turtle species of the class Reptilia occur in Turkish seas: the loggerhead sea turtle (Caretta caretta), the green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas), and the leatherback sea turtle (Dermochelys coriacea). The leatherback does not nest anywhere in the Mediterranean, and individuals sighted there are thought to originate from the Atlantic Ocean. All three species are distributed along Turkey's Mediterranean and Aegean coasts, with C. caretta and C. mydas concentrated on nesting beaches along the Mediterranean coast; there are 25 recorded sea turtle nesting beaches along the roughly 289 km of coastline between Samandag/Antakya in the east and Ekincik/Mugla in the west. Occasional records of Caretta caretta also exist from the Sea of Marmara and the Black Sea. All three species are classified as threatened by the IUCN, facing threats such as direct consumption, use of their shells or skin for ornaments, leather, or accessories, destruction of nesting, feeding, and overwintering habitat, incidental bycatch, pollution, and climate change.

Invasive Species: Species can establish themselves in a new environment for several reasons, including fouling organisms attaching to vessels, ship ballast water, fishing equipment, aquaculture and aquarium trade, and scientific research. In the Mediterranean specifically, the opening of the Suez Canal, combined with the relatively low native biodiversity of the receiving environment, has made it easier for new species to establish themselves. As a result, new species are more frequently found in lagoons used for aquaculture, in ports, and in polluted areas of the Mediterranean. The growth of alien plant and animal species in the Mediterranean has been rapid, and experts warn that at the current rate, the number of alien marine plant species could come to match or exceed native species by the mid-21st century.
