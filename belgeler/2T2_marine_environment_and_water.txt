MARINE ENVIRONMENTS AND THE WATER THAT SHAPES THEM

Life on land and in the seas has existed and changed for about 4.5 billion years. On a planet more than 70% covered by water, the organisms living in the oceans and seas, and their interactions with their surroundings, shape and form entire ecosystems. Only a very small portion of the oceans has been explored so far. The more we explore the oceans, the more we learn about the organisms that make up these ecosystems; if any one of these organisms disappears from an ecosystem, that ecosystem begins to change and may eventually collapse. Environmental factors affect life and ecosystems in the seas.

What Is Water: Water is an odorless, tasteless chemical compound essential to the survival of all living things on Earth. Although often described as colorless, it has a slightly blue tint because it weakly absorbs light at red wavelengths. Earth holds about 1.4 billion cubic kilometers of water, 97.5% of which is in liquid form. Its chemical formula is H2O: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom held together partly by cohesive forces between like molecules. Water exists in solid, liquid, and gas states, and scientists believe life on Earth began in water.

A key property of water is that its solid form (ice) is less dense than its liquid form, so ice floats rather than sinks — unlike almost every other compound, where the solid phase is denser and sinks. Water reaches its maximum density at about 4 degrees C; as it cools further below 0 degrees C, the less dense solid ice rises and the surface freezes first, insulating the water below and protecting life at depth. If this were reversed, bodies of water would freeze from the bottom up and destroy all life there.

Seawater and Its Properties: About 97% of Earth's water is in the oceans, 2.4% is glacial ice or snow, and 0.6% is in lakes and rivers. Water is an excellent solvent, and most of the 114 known chemical elements are dissolved in seawater in some quantity, though iron, titanium, zinc, copper, vanadium, bromine, boron, manganese, fluorine, aluminum, arsenic, cobalt, and radium together account for 99.7% of what gives seawater its salinity. Seawater also contains dissolved atmospheric gases, organic matter, and suspended particles of organic and inorganic origin.

Oxygen is the most important dissolved gas in seawater, essential for life, while carbon dioxide is essential for biological processes such as photosynthesis. Surface waters tend to be rich in oxygen because of photosynthesis, while deep waters tend to be poorer in oxygen due to respiration and oxidation processes. Most seawater has a salinity between 3.1% and 3.8%, though this varies globally; salinity is notably lower where fresh water mixes with seawater, such as at river mouths or near melting glaciers. The Red Sea is the saltiest open sea, due to intense evaporation, low rainfall, little river inflow, and restricted water circulation. Seawater density near the surface ranges from about 1,020 to 1,029 kg/m3 depending on temperature and salinity, and can reach 1,050 kg/m3 or higher under the extreme pressure of the deep ocean. Seawater pH is generally between 7.5 and 8.4.

Definitions of Oceans and Seas: Oceans and seas are interconnected bodies of water filling the depressions of the Earth's surface. The largest, less clearly bounded bodies of water are called oceans, while smaller, more clearly bounded portions are called seas. Large inland recesses formed by oceans and seas are called gulfs, and smaller ones are called bays. A lagoon is a body of salty or brackish water mostly separated from the sea by a coastal barrier. A strait is a passage connecting two seas, and a sill is the shallow part of such a passage. Seas bordering an ocean are called marginal seas (e.g. the English Channel and the North Sea, which have broad connections to the ocean). Seas set between continents with only a weak connection to their parent ocean, such as the Mediterranean Sea, are called inter-continental seas. Seas that open into another sea through a narrow, shallow strait, such as the Black Sea, are called inland seas. Seas with no connection to any ocean or other sea, such as the Caspian Sea, are called closed seas.
